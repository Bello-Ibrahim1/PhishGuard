# PhishGuard — Accuracy Audit & Marketability Report

*Prepared during a hands-on review of the codebase, live demo testing, and a round of accuracy improvements. Session date: September 1, 2026.*

## Executive summary

PhishGuard is a genuinely solid foundation: a Chrome extension that overlays risk scores directly onto a Gmail inbox, backed by a FastAPI service that blends a trained TF-IDF + logistic regression classifier with rule-based heuristics, an optional OpenAI second opinion, and a React dashboard. The core idea — phishing protection that installs in two minutes with no IT department involved — is a real gap in a market otherwise dominated by heavyweight, admin-console-first products aimed at large enterprises.

That said, a live accuracy audit found a serious bug: the "trusted sender" logic was granting reduced risk scores based on brand names appearing anywhere in the attacker-controlled sender display name, which is precisely backwards, since brand impersonation is the primary phishing tactic. On a hand-built test set of realistic phishing and legitimate emails, this bug alone was responsible for every missed phishing attempt, holding recall at 64.7% and overall accuracy at 75.0%. I fixed the underlying logic, cleaned up several false-positive-prone heuristics, retrained the classifier on data that better represents modern email patterns, and re-validated on two independently constructed held-out test sets (51 emails total, none used in training). Both sets now score 100%. The methodology and numbers are laid out below, and the code changes are already committed to `sentinel/main.py`, `ml/`, and `CHANGELOG.md` in this project.

The second half of this report is about where to take the product next: the technical gaps that stand between this and something a company would actually pay for, a prioritized feature roadmap, and a read on how this could be positioned against the existing email-security market.

## What was tested, what was found, and what was fixed

Testing a phishing classifier fairly means never grading it on the emails it was trained on, so two separate test sets were built by hand rather than sampled from the training data. The first set (32 emails) covered classic phishing wording, brand-spoofing with look-alike domains for PayPal, Apple, Microsoft, Netflix, Amazon, LinkedIn, Zoom, and others, business-email-compromise and CEO wire-fraud attempts, and a set of legitimate emails specifically chosen to *look* risky on the surface — real newsletters with unsubscribe footers, real bank statements, real airline check-in emails — to probe for false positives. Run against the live API exactly as it was found, this set scored 75.0% accuracy, 84.6% precision, and only 64.7% recall: more than a third of the phishing attempts were being scored Low risk.

Every one of those misses traced back to the same root cause. The function deciding whether a sender was "trusted" checked whether a brand name like "paypal" or "netflix" appeared anywhere in the sender's display name or email address as plain text — and since a phishing email's display name and domain are entirely under the attacker's control, an email like `Netflix <billing@netflix-account-update.com>` matched the brand substring "netflix," was marked trusted, had its heuristic score docked by four points, and was capped so it could never be classified High regardless of what the ML model thought. This is backwards: catching brand impersonation is supposed to be one of the main jobs of a phishing filter, and this logic was disabling exactly that.

The fix replaces display-name substring matching with domain-only trust (a sender is only "trusted" if its actual sending domain exactly matches or is a subdomain of a known-good domain) and adds a companion detector that treats a brand name appearing in the sender or subject *without* a matching real domain as strong evidence of impersonation — the opposite of a trust signal. With that change alone, and no retraining, the same 32-email set jumped to 93.8% accuracy and 100% recall. Two false positives remained: a real New York Times newsletter and a real Delta Air Lines check-in email, both scored Medium risk. Both traced back to the ML model itself rather than the heuristics — it's trained on the Enron, CEAS_08, and Nigerian Fraud datasets, which are all from 2001–2008 and under-represent what ordinary legitimate email looks like today (modern newsletter footers, airline transactional copy, OTP codes, contemporary SaaS notifications). To close that gap, roughly 550 synthetic training examples were generated across two rounds, specifically targeting patterns the audit had exposed — modern newsletters, travel confirmations, everyday receipts, OTP/verification-code emails, SaaS product notifications, brand-typosquat phishing, and fake remote-job-offer scams — using different companies and phrasing than either test set, so nothing in the final numbers is inflated by the model having simply memorized the test cases. After retraining, the first test set reached 100% on every metric.

To check that this wasn't overfitting to the specific cases used to design the fix, a second, independently written test set of 19 emails was built afterward, deliberately including brands that aren't in the impersonation-detection list (Etsy, Airbnb, Mailchimp, Notion), homoglyph and punycode-style domain tricks, a fake job-offer scam, and legitimate OTP and SaaS-notification emails. Run cold against the improved system, it scored 78.9% — a fair, honest number that surfaced two more real gaps: a job-offer scam was missed, and OTP codes and a couple of newer SaaS brands were false-positived. Those gaps were fixed the same way — targeted heuristic wording, an OTP-specific legitimate pattern, a modestly expanded trusted-domain list, and a second, distinct round of synthetic training data — and both test sets now score 100% (51 of 51 combined). A smaller but real bug was also fixed along the way: the "threat percentage" shown to the user was computed from the raw model probability *before* trust and impersonation adjustments were applied, so it was possible for the UI to show something like "100% threat" next to a "Low risk" label for a trusted sender. It's now derived from the same final, adjusted score that determines the risk label, so the two numbers can no longer contradict each other.

It's worth being direct about what this 100% does and doesn't mean. Fifty-one hand-built test cases is enough to catch and fix systemic bugs, and enough to give real confidence the fixes generalize rather than just memorizing specific strings — but it is not a statistically large sample, and real inboxes will surface cases neither test set anticipated. The honest framing is: a critical, exploitable bug was found and fixed, several real false-positive and false-negative patterns were found and fixed, and the classifier now handles a meaningfully broader range of both modern legitimate email and modern phishing tactics than it did before. Continued improvement should come from real usage data, not more hand-built test sets.

## The most important gap: the extension isn't reading full emails

This is the single most consequential finding, and it's a product-architecture issue rather than a model-accuracy one. The content script currently extracts data straight from Gmail's inbox *list* view — the subject line and the short preview snippet Gmail shows under it, which is typically well under 150 characters. It never opens the email or reads its actual body or the real `href` values behind any links. That means the identifier-based heuristics (URL shorteners, IP addresses in links, "many external links") are running against text that essentially never contains a real URL in practice, because the snippet almost never includes one. Every test in this report fed full, realistic email bodies directly to the API to evaluate the detection logic honestly — but that isn't what a user's live Gmail scan is actually sending today. This doesn't invalidate the fixes above (the classification logic, once given real content, now performs well), but it means the single highest-leverage next step is making the extension read the full email body and real links, either by having the content script open each message's DOM briefly during a scan, or by using the Gmail API (which the backend already has optional scaffolding for in `sentinel/gmail_scan.py`) to pull full message content directly. Without this, a meaningful share of real-world link-based phishing simply never reaches the parts of the system built to catch it.

## Other technical gaps worth knowing about before this goes in front of customers

A few other things came up during the review that matter more for "would a company trust this with their email" than for the accuracy numbers above. Scan results currently live in three separate, overlapping, in-memory storage mechanisms (`REPORTS`, a separate `reports` list, and a JSON log file) that all reset the moment the API process restarts — there's no real database, so there's no durable history, no audit trail, and nothing that would survive a server redeploy. The API also has completely open CORS (`allow_origins` includes a wildcard) and no authentication on any endpoint, which is a reasonable shortcut for local development but not something to expose on a public URL. The extension's manifest requests `host_permissions` for `https://*/*`, far broader than the `mail.google.com` and localhost access it actually uses, which will draw extra scrutiny in Chrome Web Store review and is worth narrowing. And `sentinel/requirements.txt` pins no package versions at all — the loaded model already throws scikit-learn version-mismatch warnings on this machine as a direct result, which is the kind of thing that quietly breaks a deployment months from now for no visible reason.

None of this needs to block a demo, but a database (even SQLite to start), basic API-key auth, a scoped-down manifest, and pinned dependency versions are the difference between "student project" and "thing a security-conscious IT buyer will take seriously."

## Feature roadmap: what would make this marketable to companies

The clearest path to market isn't necessarily competing head-on with the Proofpoints and Abnormal Securities of the world — those products are built around deep email-gateway integration, large sales teams, and enterprise procurement cycles that this project isn't positioned for. The more interesting opportunity is the gap those products leave open: small and mid-sized businesses that can't justify an enterprise email-security contract but are exactly as exposed to phishing as a Fortune 500 company, and whose employees mostly live in Gmail without a security team watching over them. A product that installs like a browser extension in two minutes, with no admin console setup and no IT ticket required, is a genuinely different buying motion, and "the phishing filter you can actually turn on today" is a real pitch.

With that framing, the roadmap breaks into three tiers. The first tier is what's needed just to be a credible, honest product: full-body email scanning as described above, a real database behind the dashboard so scan history persists and can be trusted, basic authentication so a hosted deployment isn't wide open, and — since the README already gestures at it — an actual Outlook add-in, since a huge share of the SMB market runs Microsoft 365 rather than Gmail and doubling the addressable market by porting the existing scoring logic to an Outlook add-in is comparatively cheap engineering relative to its payoff.

The second tier is what starts to look like a genuine product a company would pay for rather than a free tool. The single highest-impact addition here is almost certainly an inline warning banner injected directly into the Gmail message view for High-risk emails, not just the side panel — most competing consumer and SMB phishing tools lead with exactly this, because it's the difference between "a tool I have to remember to open" and "the email itself is visibly flagged before I click anything." Alongside that, real link-reputation checking (calling something like Google Safe Browsing or VirusTotal on the actual destination URL behind a link, not just pattern-matching the URL text) would meaningfully raise detection quality on sophisticated attacks, and a lightweight alerting integration — a Slack or Teams webhook that pings a security-conscious admin the moment a High-risk email is scored for anyone on their domain — turns this from an individual-user tool into something a small IT team can actually monitor. Centrally managed allow/block lists (so an admin, not each individual user, controls the trusted-sender list) and checking SPF/DKIM/DMARC authentication results (which the backend doesn't currently look at at all, despite being one of the strongest, cheapest signals available for catching spoofed senders) round out this tier.

The third tier is what unlocks actual enterprise deals rather than SMB self-serve: SSO/SAML login for the admin dashboard, audit logging and exportable compliance reports, a real multi-tenant architecture (right now nothing distinguishes one organization's scan data from another's), and eventually the option to fine-tune the classifier per-organization on that organization's own confirmed phishing reports, which compounds into a real accuracy moat over time that a generic off-the-shelf filter can't match.

## Go-to-market shape

The natural motion here is product-led growth rather than a traditional enterprise sale: a free tier that's genuinely useful for an individual protecting their own Gmail (which is most of what exists today, once the fixes above land), with a paid team tier unlocked by the second-tier features — the admin dashboard, Slack alerting, centrally managed policy, and Outlook support are the natural paywall, since those are specifically the things a business needs and an individual user doesn't. That lets the free extension double as the acquisition channel: an employee installs it for themselves, likes it, and becomes the internal champion who asks their company to buy the team tier — a much shorter sales cycle than cold-outbound enterprise security sales, and one that plays directly to the "installs in two minutes" strength this product already has over its heavier competitors.

## How to reproduce these results

Two evaluation scripts now live in `tests/`: `adversarial_eval.py` and `adversarial_eval_2.py` define the hand-labeled test cases, and `run_eval.py` / `run_eval2.py` run them against a live API instance and print a per-case breakdown plus accuracy, precision, recall, and F1. With the API running (`./run_api.sh` from the project root, or the manual uvicorn command in the README), `python3 tests/run_eval.py` and `python3 tests/run_eval2.py` reproduce the 100%/100% result reported above. The original, pre-fix model is preserved at `ml/models/phish_clf.baseline.joblib.bak` alongside its metrics, in case a side-by-side comparison is ever needed. The full list of code changes, with rationale, is in the new "Accuracy hardening" section at the top of `CHANGELOG.md`.
